Protocols, conferences of the parties, nationally determined contributions, carbon budgets—this is the liturgy that the scientific community has raised for decades in order to warn politicians, businesspeople, and communities while they negotiated the great human interventions on the planet. While NGOs, influencers, or local leaders seek alternatives, the global energy system forged by large investments and power projection continues its course with the parsimony of great leviathans. In the last five years something has been changing; global investment in clean energy in 2024 doubled that destined for fossil fuels.

For example, China registered its peak oil demand in 2021, has been setting aside coal-fired thermal plants—it built one per week for two decades—and began a downward trajectory. Pakistan, a state with governance problems, deployed enough solar capacity to generate surplus liquefied natural gas in less than three years. In Africa, Ethiopia banned the import of internal combustion vehicles while its economy grew 70%, and many other data points admit several readings. The one that interests me most proposes that the global energy transformation responds to a logic that multilateral regulatory frameworks only partially capture.

What happened is the first thing one might ask. It was not morality for the planet, nor declarations of good will at successive green conferences, nor the climate crisis, but rather the structural forces that pushed a change. The objective thermodynamic advantages of certain technologies, combined with competition for strategic positioning in an increasingly fragmented international system, generate dynamics that operate with relative independence from formal climate commitments.

![Door of opportunity to change](/images/posts/puerta-oportunidad-cambio.webp)
*A green opportunity that opens*

It is worth recalling that the relationship between energy and state power generates its own geography of power and its own fragilities. The transition to oil led by the United States during and after the Second World War was a complete reengineering of the international order and reached its high point in the 1973 crisis. In recent decades, the cost structure and strategic advantages associated with different energy vectors changed. Fossil fuel-based technologies face cumulative losses while photovoltaic and wind technologies operate under the logic of marginal costs close to zero post-installation, absence of fuel supply chains, and steep learning curves. Their manufacturing, moreover, is scalable and relocatable, without dependence on specific geographic endowments.

China identified already in the Ninth Five-Year Plan (1996-2000) that clean energy technologies represented a historic opportunity to overcome inherited disadvantages. Strong green policies, tinged with Western disbelief for a long time, turned the country into a power that controls 60% of global polysilicon manufacturing, 85% of rare earth processing, and 77% of global lithium-ion battery capacity. That race for self-sufficiency determined which economies should capture technological rents, skilled employment, and the capacity to influence technical standards for decades.

The pursuit of strategic autonomy came from geopolitical fragmentation and from each country's political urgency. Pakistan offers a revealing case very far from the idealization of Mother Nature. Each solar megawatt installed avoids imports valued at 2-3 million dollars annually and preserves scarce foreign currency. The climate aspect, let us say it plainly, is a fortunate collateral benefit. India faces its own strategic nightmare when it is forced to import more than 85% of its crude oil, and a good part transits maritime routes such as the Strait of Hormuz and the Arabian Sea. Calling it 'climate policy' would be picturesque. It is elementary reduction of strategic vulnerability in the face of a conflict scenario such as the one Iran has with the United States, to go no further.

The Persian Gulf States (Saudi Arabia, United Arab Emirates, Qatar, etc.) have economies that depend on the rent they obtain from selling oil and gas, not on producing goods or services with their own labor and technology. This is key since they make a cold calculation because they know that oil will run out as the dominant source, and they want to reposition themselves so as not to lose power when that happens. Only thus will they be able to remain influential in the new world of clean energies, just as they were in the oil world. That is why they invest in renewables, lithium, cobalt, graphite, etc. They do not do it to save the planet, but to preserve power and influence. That it coincides with climate objectives is a coincidence, and a good one.

### Solving one problem opens others.

During three centuries of industrialization environmental costs did not enter the balance of progress, and now ecological repair generates its own externalities, and the most visible is the displacement of local livelihoods. Even the word "sustainability" has been absorbed by the lexicon of growth. In the Andean zones of Peru there is a rich body of restoration practices underway, whose core is to rehabilitate the water and agricultural infrastructures of the pre-Inca era, including mainly andenes (agricultural terraces), qochas (infiltration ponds), and infiltration canals. These projects are being promoted at the national level by Peru's Ministry of Agrarian Development and Irrigation and Agro Rural, with a logic that highly coincides with works that functioned before and function again now.

![Subak system of Bali, World Heritage since 2012](/images/posts/subak-bali.webp)
*Painting of the Subak, an 11th-century Indonesian method for managing and irrigating rice terraces*

The Subak system of Bali, recognized as a World Heritage Site by UNESCO in 2012, has been operational for more than a thousand years. A study published in Environmental Earth Sciences found that optimizing its traditional water reuse mechanism—the "natak tiyis"—without modifying the physical structure increases water availability and expands the irrigation area downstream. I know no better example of the logic of "not remaking the old, but using it again."

Some sites inspire a reverence that curbs the extractive impulse; others ecologically comparable do not. The difference lies in accumulated practice: norms of containment, pilgrimage rituals and offerings that bind the community to a common calendar, origin stories that turn the place into ancestral debt. The sacred, in that register, functions as a technology for managing common goods, and is underused if it is not transferred to public policies. It is a tool beyond the norm and the incentive.

Returning species and processes to territories from which human activity removed them implies recognizing that those places have a title of their own, beyond their utility. It does not change the logic where marginal costs, supply chains, and strategic positioning will continue to operate with or without regulatory consensus, but only thus, through technologies, geopolitics, local tradition, and revaluation of territories, is the energy transition gaining ground.
