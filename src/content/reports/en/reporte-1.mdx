# Introduction: Executive Summary

Three central aspects to consider:

1. **Investment Volume:** The net volume of investment committed by Chinese companies in Peru and Chile over the past year (2025/2026) is approximately USD 13.030 billion, with some 10 announced investment projects, some partially executed and others in the pre-operational phase.
2. **Impact on Commodity Costs:** The implications for agricultural and mining companies have been various modifications that have impacted their cost structure. This is explained in part because the South Pacific port nodes (San Antonio, Chancay, and Callao) have shown an increase in physical capacity as a direct result of the trade foreign policy of Asia-Pacific countries and their need to reduce logistics costs in increasingly extensive and vulnerable supply chains. In addition, *chokepoints* have varied their traffic levels with the conflict between Israel and the United States with Iran, which has led to the closure of the Strait of Hormuz and impacts oil prices given that 20% of global trade transits through said strait. This has been a challenge for agricultural and mining companies, considering the threat of transnational organized crime affecting ports in the Asia-Pacific region.
3. **Geoeconomic Strategy:** The trade policy of Asia-Pacific countries operates through economic integration agreements and free trade treaties, which not only seek to open markets but also to physically redesign the map of maritime trade to lower costs and secure the flow of goods. Investment in port and rail infrastructure in the South Pacific, route diversification, and threats such as transnational organized crime and instability in global *chokepoints* converge on the same logic: reducing the volatility of export costs and increasing the resilience of the supply chains that feed their industrial and digital economies.

# Section 1: Geopolitical and Institutional Context, and Actor Profiles

Currently, the international order is experiencing the consequences of the geopolitical dispute between China and the United States. Along these lines, the Asia-Pacific region has gradually been acquiring prominence on the international stage and especially in international trade. For years, Asian economies have grown and gained prominence in terms of trade exchange, particularly in consumption and raw materials, while facing as their Achilles' heel the enormous distance separating commodity production sources from the various processing centers.

## Peru: Strategic Role and Insertion

Peru is a key connection point within regional maritime trade thanks to four fundamental pillars:

* **Regional logistics hub:** Strengthened by the Port of Callao (which concentrates the majority of container and general cargo movement) and by the Port of Chancay, a megaproject designed to position the country as the logistics axis that will attract trade flows directly from Asia-Pacific.
* **Export support:** Infrastructure oriented toward key industries such as agro-exports, fishing, and mining.
* **Investment attraction:** Modernization process based on the port concession model.
* **Connectivity and territorial integration:** Reflected in river terminals such as Yurimaguas, which enable integration from the Amazon.

With a climate of political instability and a constantly growing macroeconomy, Peru has become a relevant actor given its strategic location and the Chancay megaport project. The country has consolidated a global trade insertion thanks to a network of 23 trade agreements in force covering between 87% and 91% of its total exports, among which the FTA with China, the agreements with the United States and the European Union, membership in the CPTPP (a bloc to which the United Kingdom acceded in 2024), and the FTA between the Pacific Alliance and Singapore, in force since May 2025, stand out.

## Chile: Strategic Role and Trade Openness

Chile's strategic strength is the lithium triangle and access to other critical minerals for the global energy transition, which has generated tensions with the United States, for whom Chinese investments in Latin America generate sensitivity, particularly in telecommunications, ports, and critical minerals.

Chile is an economy open to the world through an integration process via free trade agreements that began to be negotiated following the return to democracy in 1990. Chile has currently signed 34 FTAs, in addition to its accession to APEC in 1993, the creation of the Pacific Alliance in 2011, and the ratification of the CPTPP in 2022.

As a country open to the global economy, Chile has China and the United States as its two most important trading partners. Since the entry into force of the FTA with China in 2006, this country has been the main destination for Chilean exports. Infrastructure projects operate under a public-private collaboration format through concessions, deepening trade ties.

## Asia-Pacific Actor Profiles

* **China:** A constantly growing country that disputes global leadership with the United States, with a dominant position in the extraction and processing of critical minerals such as rare earths, manganese, graphite, lithium, cobalt, and aluminum. Its industry manufactures 75% of all batteries in the world and the majority of electric vehicles globally.
* **Singapore:** Has consolidated as a global leader in the digital economy through a comprehensive strategy combining public policies, international agreements, and technological innovation, with more than 30 submarine cable projects and one of the largest ports in the world. It is a member of the WTO, APEC, and ASEAN.
* **Hong Kong:** An open economy characterized by its technology hubs and banking networks, consolidated as a key piece in the trade exchange of mainland China with other countries, being a member of the World Trade Organization and APEC.

## Detailed Project Breakdown

* **San Antonio Outer Port (Chile):** Public-private investment of US$ 4.450 billion. Expansion and modernization of the port system through a breakwater of ~4 km, dredging, and platforms for two terminals of 1,730 m in four stages. It will triple transfer capacity to handle eight post-Panamax vessels of 400 m in length (6 million TEU annually). TEU movement grew 2.3% year-over-year to 682,939 units at the close of Q1 2026. Pre-qualified companies: Dragados-Sacyr, CRCC, Van Oord, Jan de Nul, CHEC, Hyundai E&C, Acciona-Deme.
* **Chancay Megaport (Peru):** Estimated investment of US$ 3.500 billion. The first phase (US$ 1.300B) enabled operational port infrastructure. The second phase (2027) contemplates US$ 2.500B. The Chancay-Shanghai route handled a total of 78,000 tons of goods for 240.76 million dollars in the first half of 2025 (+34.9% year-over-year). The journey takes 23 days, reducing logistics costs by more than 20% for agro-exports.
* **Callao Port Terminal (Peru):** Investment of US$ 550 million in expansion by APM Terminals at the end of 2025. DP World executed the "Bicentennial Pier" for US$ 400 million, mobilizing 396,429 TEU in its first year (+24.07%).

# Section 3: Inference Analysis and Strategic Discussion

## 3.1. Reconfiguration of Economic Positioning

The strategic effect of the Port of Chancay rests on Peru's position within global supply chains. The initial investment of USD 1.300 billion in Chancay, the majority shareholding of Cosco Shipping Ports (60%), and the reduction of approximately 10 days in maritime transit times between Peru and Asia contribute to the country's competitiveness. With the port's incorporation into the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI), Peru acts as a regional node connecting trade flows between Latin America and Asia.

For Chile, the challenge of logistics competitiveness arises from the time difference: Callao and Chancay are approximately 5 days faster than San Antonio and Valparaíso, but it is illogical for cargo from Chile to be unloaded and reloaded in Callao or Chancay before being exported to Asia, as this would block the capacity of Peruvian ports and increase times again. The ports of Arica and Iquique are within Chancay's logistics network and complement each other in the transit of goods: for Iquique, Chancay is an opportunity for regional integration, although there is competition between Callao/Chancay and San Antonio/Valparaíso where exports are of similar products.

## 3.2. Evaluation of Key Port Projects

This section analyzes port investment projects, Asia-Pacific maritime routes, and the role of transnational organized crime in port security, revisiting in detail the three central nodes: San Antonio, Chancay, and Callao, described in Section 2.

## 3.3. Transformation of Diplomatic-Trade Relations

For Peru, the strengthening of China-driven infrastructure does not imply a replacement of economic relations with the United States; Peru becomes a strategic logistics asset for transpacific trade. The development of Chancay modifies diplomatic-trade relations by being financed and operated by Chinese capital, which implies competition to maintain economic influence over a logistics node and increases Peru's capacity to negotiate with multiple international actors.

For Chile, there has been greater geopolitical friction with the United States over Chinese investments. The Chile-China Express submarine cable project has sparked parliamentary debate and reservations in Western blocs over data transfer security. In parallel, the penetration of Chinese state capital into the electricity transmission and distribution network (via State Grid) places Chile under greater critical infrastructure security scrutiny.

Both countries have taken adaptation measures: Peru enacted Law No. 32706 (July 2026) to reactivate cabotage, while Chile approved Law No. 21.774 to promote competition in cabotage transport. In terms of governance and cybersecurity, Chile strengthened its institutional framework through Law No. 21.663 (Cybersecurity Framework Law) and the National Cybersecurity Agency (ANCI), in line with the 2023-2028 national cybersecurity policy and its permanent institutional framework, while Peru maintains its regulatory focus on tariff oversight (Ositrán vs. Cosco Shipping) and environmental enforcement. An additional bill seeks to review strategic foreign direct investment (FDI) in Chile.

## 3.4. Redesign of Maritime Routes and Their Impact on Commodities

The inauguration of the direct Chancay-Shanghai corridor reduced transit times to 23 days and lowered logistics costs by more than 20%. While this benefits perishable agro-industrial products, the impact on mining commodities (copper and lithium) operates under a different logic: in mining, the critical factors are freight rates per ton, the availability of bulk carriers, and resilience against global *chokepoints*.

Copper and lithium are exported in large volumes and their value does not deteriorate due to additional days; the key indicator is the logistics cost measured in USD per ton transported, which can represent between 8% and 15% of the FOB value. The direct corridor reduces intermediate stops, port congestion, and waiting surcharges, lowering the cost per ton and stabilizing transport rates. Resilience against strategic *chokepoints*—Malacca, Hormuz, and Bab el-Mandeb—is fundamental: the Chancay-Shanghai corridor contributes to reducing dependence on Malacca, enabling alternative routes through Lombok or Sunda.

In parallel, Panama Canal restrictions, the rise of the Strait of Magellan, and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz drive a reconfiguration of regional nodes to reduce transfer costs. Peru seeks to position itself as a regional hub, while Chile proposes strengthening its position through the expansion of San Antonio. These transformations imply shorter times and greater resilience, although they increase dependence on Asian logistics chains and deepen the geopolitical competition between China and the United States.

## 3.5. Opportunities for Companies and Investors

The economic impact of the Port of Chancay generates opportunities for complementary investments in storage, land transport, industrial parks, distribution centers, and digital solutions. The coexistence of Chancay and Callao favors a redistribution of cargo traffic that can improve the efficiency of the national logistics system.

For Chile, the opportunity lies in accelerating the operational efficiency and intermodal connectivity (rail and road) agenda in San Antonio and Valparaíso to mitigate the sailing time difference, and in advancing the bill on the review of strategic foreign direct investment (FDI). For the regional ecosystem, the relationship should be approached under specialization: Peru as a transpacific transshipment node and Chile focused on the export of critical minerals and the integration of bioceanic corridors. Iquique can be a redistribution point for northern Chile, Bolivia, and Argentina, opening possibilities for strategic alliances with Chancay operators and even integrated tourism synergies.

## 3.6. Transnational Organized Crime and Port Security

Transnational organized crime has been gaining prominence in Asia-Pacific, using ports to increase its illicit operations. By 2011, the region did not have a consensus definition of organized crime, but over time forums such as the ASEAN Action Plan (2022-2026) and ASEANPOL were created. The APEC Forum includes anti-corruption working groups, and the PTCN coordinates island countries in cooperation against drug trafficking.

* **Drug trafficking:** In 2021 it reached record levels (172 tons seized) in the "golden triangle." The Port of San Antonio was considered by UNODC in 2023 as a key port for transit to Europe and North America.
* **Human trafficking:** An estimated 29 million people live under exploitation in Asia-Pacific (58% of global activity).
* **Migrant smuggling:** With greater incidence in Myanmar and Sri Lanka.
* **Wildlife trafficking:** The pangolin was the most trafficked species between 2002 and 2019.
* **Corruption:** The region has experienced stagnation in the fight against corruption, with an average of 45 points out of 100.
* **Cybercrime:** In 2022, Asia-Pacific concentrated 31% of the world's cyberattacks.

The Global Organized Crime Index 2025 highlights that synthetic drugs are changing the landscape due to their profitability; Southeast Asia is a major center of methamphetamine production, while Pacific countries serve as transit points. The Amazon region faces the expansion of Ecuadorian gangs into Peru, where the fight against trafficking is hampered by political instability.

In terms of port security, by 2017 the region concentrated 42% of global container maritime traffic. The recruitment of young multilingual professionals for cybercrimes through fake job offers is documented; victims are subjected to severe punishments and exploitation in scam centers. In Chile's case, the "random cargo" and "targeted cargo" scheme (Table No. 3) affects the capacity to combat organizations effectively. Chile collaborates with UNODC in the Global Container Program; in 2022 it seized chemical precursors sufficient to produce up to 60 tons of cocaine. The figure is part of a larger pattern of historic precursor seizures in the country. Meanwhile, Callao functions as a strategic point for the export of cocaine and counterfeit products to Europe. Ecuador, with the ports of Esmeraldas and Guayaquil, has become the country with the second-most seizures in the world, facing a 429.8% increase in homicides due to competition between criminal gangs. Port security remains, according to local analysts, a neglected link in the agenda to combat organized crime.

## 3.7. Conclusions and Geostrategic Perspective

The reconfiguration of South Pacific port nodes (San Antonio, Chancay, and Callao) is the direct result of the trade foreign policy of Asia-Pacific countries and their need to reduce logistics costs in increasingly extensive and vulnerable chains. China, Singapore, and Hong Kong have consolidated strategies combining infrastructure investment, control of critical routes, and deepening of trade agreements to secure the flow of *commodities*.

China reduces its exposure to traditional *chokepoints* by diversifying corridors that lower transport costs. The direct Chancay-Shanghai route reduces costs by more than 20%, reinforcing the competitiveness of countries capable of integrating into these preferential corridors. Chile's trade foreign policy operates as an enabling framework for Asian investment to materialize in concessioned infrastructure, offering regulatory certainty to East Asian corporations seeking to reduce transport costs.

In parallel, the emergence of alternative routes such as Lombok and Sunda responds to the need to reduce dependence on single corridors and stabilize costs in conflict scenarios. The organized crime dimension introduces an additional component in the cost structure—port security and reputational risk—that forces States to internalize mitigation costs. In synthesis, these projects should be read as geostrategic instruments: by integrating, Chile and Peru insert themselves into a power architecture where export costs are determined by investment and risk management decisions made in the major Asian decision centers.
