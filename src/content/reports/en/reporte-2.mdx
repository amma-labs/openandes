# Introduction: Executive Summary

Three aspects condition investment in ports and mining corridors of the South American Pacific:

**First**, the geopolitical dispute between China and the United States has conditioned the foreign policy of Latin American countries. In the technological field, the dispute has centered on replicating the logic of networks and alliances through dominance of the chokepoints of the technology chain, limiting the development options of the other power. And this dispute also affects Latin America, mainly because China has consolidated itself as the leading trading partner of the South American countries, displacing the United States to second place, betting on increasing its influence in the region, expressed in China's white paper on global governance, entitled "A Fair and Equitable Global Governance: China's Principles, Proposals and Actions," and the United States' 2025 National Security Strategy. In defense matters, Latin American countries have been rearming and strengthening their military capabilities as deterrents, which has generated diplomatic tensions among the countries of the region. Nevertheless, the countries cooperate in the Santiago Commitment and the Shield of the Americas.

**Second**, at the electoral level, Latin American countries are experiencing a shift to the right based on issues such as security and the economy to confront transnational organized crime and reduce the role of the state apparatus in terms of regulations, with the aim of attracting investment. This phenomenon occurs after the "red tide" process that implied the predominance of left-wing governments with an agenda of social justice and combating inequality that implied an increase in regulations. This is reflected in the electoral pendulum that the region has experienced and in the internal conflictivity with administrations that have failed to resolve the main problems afflicting their fellow citizens.

**Third**, the threat of transnational organized crime is installed as a problem that affects the capacities of the State and its security. This threat stands as a relevant non-state actor in South America, with the power to control territories, co-opt strategic ports, hinder the actions of regional armed forces and of public or private investment through violence and the deployment of institutional co-optation tactics. Investments that can affect strategic projects and the institutional dimension that includes stakeholders in processes of social conflictivity. This is added to the geopolitical dimension of the growing relevance of maritime cooperation and commercial route security mechanisms in the Asia-Pacific space. The increase in exports of critical minerals to Asian markets has raised the strategic importance of the ports and logistics corridors of the South American west coast.

The specific port nodes that require capital investment (CAPEX) are three: **Matarani** (structural saturation due to copper projects), **Callao** (critical gap for the realization of new projects) and **Antofagasta/Mejillones** (pressure from Argentine projects dependent on Chilean ports). For Foreign Direct Investment, project financing structures backed by multilateral institutions reduce risk for investors, improve the project's bankability and position assets with secured off-takers as priority candidates for M&A, such as the Rincón lithium project. This within the framework of a process of vertical logistics integration in which both foreign funds and companies can capture value through collection centers, dedicated corridors and participation in specialized terminals.

# Section 1: Geopolitical and Institutional Context, and Actor Profiles

In the case of China, it is possible to note that it has projected its influence in recent decades through trade and infrastructure, with the Belt and Road as its most visible expression. In AI it follows an analogous path, as it seeks to expand through mass adoption. In July 2025 it presented its Action Plan for Global AI Governance, oriented toward the Global South through digital infrastructure, technology transfer and participation in international standards. These initiatives are explained from the perspective that it aspires to fulfill its central objective identified as becoming the world's leading power by 2049, which implies, for the Asia-Pacific case, confronting the interests of many other States, among them those of the U.S. oriented toward keeping maritime routes open, including the Taiwan Strait. In pursuit of this objective, China has deepened its trade ties with Latin America, specifically with South America, displacing the United States. China's rise as a trading power has had profound implications for world trade over the last 20 years, with key economic sectors of Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC) among the main beneficiaries. Between 2000 and 2020, trade between China and LAC multiplied by 26, going from US$12 billion to US$315 billion. In addition, China consolidated itself as a lender to the region for obtaining credits and support in infrastructure projects: loans granted through China's development financial institutions total, taking 2005 to 2022 as a reference, US$136 billion, with Argentina, Brazil, Ecuador and Venezuela being the main recipients.

In this context of growing global demand and technological expansion driven by the great powers, South American countries have begun to articulate strategic responses to position themselves in the global value chain. Thus, on August 28, Chile, Bolivia, Argentina and Peru signed a joint declaration to promote the development of the critical minerals industry. The purpose of the recent signing lies in advancing the responsible and sustainable development of mining and promoting the region as a strategic and reliable partner to supply critical minerals to industries such as electromobility and artificial intelligence (AI). This push aims to consolidate the Southern Cone as a strategic supplier of these resources. In the case of lithium, the global market has been experiencing significant growth in recent years due to the growing demand for lithium-ion batteries for electric vehicles and renewable energy storage systems. Batteries continue to be the main end use of lithium, representing over 87%, and their demand is estimated to continue growing hand in hand with the boom in electric vehicle sales and energy storage systems. Other end uses of lithium are ceramics and glass (4%), lubricating greases (2%), air treatment (1%), pharmaceuticals (1%), among others.

It should be added that one of the reasons that encouraged the joint declaration among the governments of the four signatory countries lies in the exponential advances in the technological and energy field. These advances imply that in the future the energy transition and artificial intelligence infrastructure will demand between 400% and 600% more critical minerals in the next decade, which requires strengthening the stability of global supplies. Likewise, it highlighted that exports of mining goods and services to neighboring countries far exceed the direct exchange of minerals, proposing the expansion of the Mining Integration and Complementarity Treaty with Argentina and the memoranda of understanding with Peru as models for accelerating investments. This decision is aligned with the region's strategic interests in terms of the lithium and copper supply chain in the Andean corridor through the strengthening of regional cooperation.

## Peru: strategic role and insertion

Peru is a key connection point within regional maritime trade thanks to four fundamental pillars. The first is its positioning as a Regional Logistics Hub oriented toward the Pacific, which allows it to be the main maritime connection center of the area, and this is strengthened mainly by the Port of Callao, which concentrates most of the container and general cargo movement, and by the Port of Chancay, a megaproject designed to position the country as the logistics axis that will attract commercial flows directly from Asia-Pacific. Both operate under Law 27943 of the National Port System, which distinguishes between terminals for public use and for private use. That distinction determines who supervises and who sets tariffs.

The second axis is consolidated through support for the export of key industries such as agro-exports, fishing and mining. The Peruvian framework offers legal stability to the investor through Legislative Decrees 662 and 757 (1991), as well as mining stability contracts, which set the tax regime and other conditions for determined periods, and which the Constitution protects by recognizing the intangibility of contracts in its article 62.

As a third axis, the attraction of investments and modernization stands out, visible in the port concession model through the promotion of private investment, ranging from docks to automated systems and advanced logistics technology to improve competitiveness against other international markets. They are governed by the Public-Private Partnerships (PPP) framework through Legislative Decree No. 1362 and its regulations. The Constitution guarantees in its article 63 equal treatment between national and foreign investment and allows disputes to be submitted to national or international arbitration. Peru has been a party to ICSID (International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes) since 1993, and its investment treaties and investment chapters of FTAs grant the investor access to arbitration against the State. An example was the Tza Yap Shum v. Republic of Peru case.

Finally, the fourth pillar comprises internal connectivity and territorial cohesion, reflected in terminals located in the Peruvian jungle where, through river transport, facilities such as Yurimaguas allow integration from the Amazon.

The country has consolidated a global trade insertion thanks to a network of 23 trade agreements in force that cover between 87% and 91% of its total exports. Among the most relevant treaties are the FTA with China, the agreements with the United States and the European Union, and its membership in the CPTPP, a bloc to which the United Kingdom acceded in 2024; to this is added the FTA between the Pacific Alliance and Singapore, in force since May 2025.

In port matters, the country has 60 ports in total: 46 located on the coast and 14 on inland rivers, of which 17 are for public use and 43 for private use. During 2025 more than 132 million metric tonnes were moved, led by the Port of Callao with more than 42 million tonnes between the South Zone and North Multipurpose container terminals. In containers, Callao moved close to 3.07 million TEU in 2024, which placed it as the fourth most important port in Latin America according to ECLAC, and 3.3 million TEU in 2025. It is followed by Shougang Hierro Perú, a private port in Ica, with 17.5 million tonnes, and Matarani with 8.4 million. The Chancay megaport moved 3 million tonnes in 2025 and 502,646 TEU in its first year of commercial operation, through May 2026, and it is projected that it could reach 3.5 million TEU by 2032.

At the institutional level, the governing bodies of maritime transport and cabotage are the Ministry of Transport and Communications (MTC), the National Port Authority (APN) and OSITRAN as tariff regulator, in addition to DICAPI, which exercises maritime authority and belongs to the Peruvian Navy. Regarding the legal framework, Law No. 32706 (published on July 4, 2026) stands out, which modernizes national cabotage and grants tax incentives, complemented by Legislative Decree 1413 and Law 28583.

## Chile: strategic role and trade openness

Chile's strategic strength is its position in the lithium triangle, alongside Argentina and Bolivia, and its access to other critical minerals for the global energy transition. That is why tensions have arisen with the United States, for whom Chinese investments in Latin America generate sensitivity, particularly in the case of telecommunications, ports and critical minerals. The last controversial project was the Chile-China Express, the submarine cable between Valparaíso and Hong Kong, but geopolitical tension may reappear with the tender for the Port of San Antonio. Its expansion project contemplates an estimated investment of US$4,450 million and some of the prequalified companies are China Harbour Engineering Company Ltd and the China Railway Construction Corporation (CRCC) consortium. In July 2026, the San Antonio Port Company (EPSA) annulled the tender for the outer port and announced that it would reformulate the megaproject's strategies after a study by the Chilean Maritime Port Chamber that questioned the need to build the port before 2045 and suggested that improving the efficiency of existing terminals and expanding the Port of Valparaíso could prove more viable.

At the legal level, Chile's openness policy rests on three layers. The first is trade treaties: Chile has an FTA with China (in force since 2006, with a supplementary investment agreement from 2012) and with the United States (2004), and has been part of the CPTPP since 2023, a bloc to which China does not belong but to which it has requested accession. Since the entry into force of the FTA with China, that country has been the main destination for Chilean exports. In the first half of 2026, Chile's trade exchange reached US$106,498 million, and goods exports accumulated a return of US$60,354 million. The main destinations were China (33.9%) and the United States (16.7%). Of the total exported, mining represents 61.1%, and lithium was triple that of last year (US$3,218 million).

The second layer is investment protection: Chile is a party to ICSID, and its FTAs and investment treaties grant access to international arbitration. The third is the domestic investment law, governed by Law 20,848, which replaced DL 600 and created the promotion institutionality (InvestChile); that law does not establish national security filters. That is why, in March 2026, a bill was presented to create a mechanism for reviewing strategic investments, which is still under consideration.

The Chilean port system is governed by Law 19,542, on the modernization of the state port sector, which creates the state port companies and regulates concessions to private operators. The Port of San Antonio is the tenth most important in Latin America. During 2025, San Antonio moved 2,060,244 TEU. It has a strategic location with the main highways and the intermodal rail connection. It is state-owned through EPSA, and is administered by private concessions. Its master plan contemplates an "Outer Port," whose first stage will be completed around 2036 with a capacity of 1.5 million TEU per year. The Port of Valparaíso is administered by the Valparaíso Port Company (EPV), owned by the Chilean State, but maintains three concession contracts with private companies that operate specific terminals. The ports of Arica and Iquique are integrated into the Chancay logistics network through the Chancay Express feeder service, so they do not compete. The Port of Antofagasta concentrates on mining, not on trans-Pacific container traffic, and its main commitment is to the bioceanic corridor. The governance of Chilean state port companies is governed by OECD standards.

## Actor profiles: Argentina and Bolivia

**Argentina:** After coming to power in 2023, the administration of Javier Milei has developed a policy of economic openness and favorable to sustained foreign investment. Along these lines, the government has promoted the approval of projects through the Incentive Regime for Large Investments (RIGI). The regime, created by Law 27,742 and regulated by Decree 749/2024, targets projects of at least US$200 million and offers tax, customs and exchange stability for 30 years, in addition to access to international arbitration. This regime also involves investments in mining projects for the extraction of lithium, copper and gold, whose increase in investments has implied having the largest amount of mining investment under this scheme, with more than US$13,400 million. This materializes with mining projects (for example the Vicuña Project) in the Josemaría and Filo del Sol deposits. Despite this, in legal terms, RIGI presents risks that could imply eliminating the incentive regime in 2027, which would affect new mining investments and the development of local suppliers in Argentina.

Argentina depends on Chilean Pacific ports for lithium and copper projects such as Olaroz, Pozuelos, Centenario-Ratones and Taca Taca. Argentina is not a landlocked State, so it does not enjoy the right of transit of UNCLOS (arts. 124 to 132), and its access relies on bilateral treaties with Chile, such as the 1984 Treaty of Peace and Friendship and the 1997 Mining Integration and Complementarity Treaty. Those treaties facilitate transit and binational projects, but they are not equivalent to a right of preferential access. Its political closeness to Washington coexists with its trade dependence on China.

**Bolivia:** Bolivia has experienced a shift in its foreign policy by making changes to its economic and development model after Rodrigo Paz assumed power in October 2025. This change has consisted of greater flexibilization and elimination of regulations under the proposal called "Capitalism for All" during the campaign period. From that context, the government has presented a bill that contemplates a series of structural reforms of 103 articles with three fundamental axes. The first axis proposes to "normalize" private capital, because "today investment is the atypical fact in Bolivia," said the Minister of Economy, José Gabriel Espinoza, at a press conference. According to the government, the percentage of foreign direct investment in other countries of the region ranges between 20% and 30% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP), while in Bolivia it does not exceed 2% or 3%. The second and third axes consist mainly of creating a system of incentives and regulation that allows giving security, stability and confidence to the investor. This with the aim of proceeding with the strategic bills for the exploitation of hydrocarbons, mining, electricity and lithium.

Bolivia is, moreover, a landlocked State. It has the right of access to the sea and freedom of transit (UNCLOS, arts. 124 to 132), supported by the 1904 Treaty with Chile and the 1992 Ilo Convention with Peru. In 2018, the International Court of Justice ruled that Chile did not have the obligation to negotiate sovereign access. Its cargo therefore depends on Arica, Antofagasta and Ilo.

# Section 3: Inference Analysis and Strategic Discussion

## 3.1. Reconfiguration of economic positioning

Chancay seeks to reposition Peru in global supply chains: from an exporter of raw materials with departure ports to the logistics node with direct connection to Asia. The initial investment of USD 1,300 million in Chancay, the majority shareholding of Cosco Shipping Ports (60%) and the reduction of around 10 days in maritime transit times between Peru and Asia contribute to the country's competitiveness. With the incorporation of the port into the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI), Peru acts as a regional node connecting commercial flows between Latin America and Asia.

For Chile, the challenge is one of logistics competitiveness: Callao and Chancay are about five days closer to Asia than San Antonio and Valparaíso. However, unloading and reloading Chilean cargo at Peruvian ports before exporting it to Asia is not an obvious solution, because it would compete for capacity with Peruvian cargo, congest the terminals and nullify the time advantage.

More than displacing Chile, Chancay partially integrates it. Arica and Iquique are part of its logistics network through the Chancay Express feeder service and complement each other in the transit of goods, which for Iquique represents an opportunity for regional integration. This service is international maritime transport between two States, so it does not constitute cabotage. Chilean ports also serve Bolivian cargo in accordance with the 1904 Treaty, while Peru offers Bolivia the port of Ilo through the 1992 Convention, so that integration with Chancay expands Bolivia's logistics alternatives. Direct competition between Callao/Chancay and San Antonio/Valparaíso exists only when the export offering is similar, for example in fresh fruit for export to Asian markets, and not in minerals, since the bulk of Chile's copper cargo is moved mainly through the ports of the country's north (such as Antofagasta or Mejillones). The result is an asymmetric reconfiguration: Peru gains positioning as a hub, while Chile retains its mining cargo but must close the time gap with operational efficiency and intermodal connectivity.

## 3.2. Mine-to-port logistics configuration and bottlenecks

The data collection shows that the logistics chain of the Andean corridor presents critical asymmetries between projects, distances and port nodes. In Peru, mining flows are concentrated in three main ports: Matarani, Callao and Punta Lobitos. These absorb cargo from projects with distances ranging between 100 km (Cerro Verde) and 780 km (Las Bambas). This dispersion generates operational stress and dependence on extensive corridors, increasing the probability of interruptions due to road failures, social blockades or climatic restrictions.

In Argentina, dependence on Chilean ports (Antofagasta, Mejillones, Angamos) for lithium and copper projects (such as Olaroz, Pozuelos, Centenario-Ratones and Taca Taca) evidences a structural cross-border interdependence that conditions operational continuity and export competitiveness. This dependence implies that any interruption at Chilean ports directly affects the operational continuity of Argentine projects.

Peruvian public ports show relevant gaps:

- **Matarani:** saturation due to multiple copper projects.
- **Callao:** limited capacity for concentrates and dependence on a single specialized terminal.
- **Salaverry:** insufficient growth to absorb new cargo.

In contrast, private terminals (Shougang, Miski Mayo), according to the table, concentrate more than 72% of total mining cargo, which generates competitive rigidities for new entrants and limits logistics diversification.

## 3.3. Transformation of diplomatic-commercial relations

For Peru, the strengthening of infrastructure driven by China does not imply a replacement of economic relations with the United States, with which it maintains a trade promotion agreement in force since 2009. On the contrary, Peru becomes a strategic logistics asset for trans-Pacific trade. The development of Chancay modifies Peru's diplomatic-commercial relations by being financed and operated by Chinese capital, which implies competition to maintain economic influence over a logistics node. Competition for investments, technological cooperation and complementary infrastructure is expected, and with it, Peru increases its capacity to negotiate with multiple international actors.

For Chile there has been greater geopolitical friction with the United States due to Chinese investments in the country. The Chile-China Express submarine cable project (Valparaíso-Hong Kong) has sparked a parliamentary debate and objections in Western blocs over the security of data transfer. And in parallel, the penetration of Chinese state capital into the electricity transmission and distribution network (via State Grid, whose purchase of CGE was approved by the FNE in March 2021) places Chile under greater critical infrastructure security scrutiny.

To adapt to this, both countries have taken different measures:

- **Cabotage and Market Opening:** both countries have activated legislative reforms to dynamize maritime transport. While Peru enacted Law No. 32706 (July 2026) to reactivate cabotage and tax incentives, Chile approved Law No. 21774, amending the merchant marine framework to promote competition in cabotage transport.
- **Governance and Cybersecurity:** in response to alerts about strategic infrastructure, Chile strengthened its institutional framework through Law No. 21663 (Cybersecurity Framework Law) and the creation of the National Cybersecurity Agency (ANCI), while Peru maintains its regulatory focus on tariff supervision (Ositrán vs. Cosco Shipping) and environmental enforcement (OEFA/SPDA).

## 3.4. Political-institutional risk and organized crime in the corridors

The analysis of logistics corridors cannot be limited to physical infrastructure. The continuity and expansion of mining projects also depend on the quality of public institutions, permit management and coordination between the different levels of government. In this sense, state capacity, clarity of rules and predictability of decisions are key factors for attracting investment, especially in long-term projects that require multiple authorizations.

Likewise, a delay in a permit does not always mean administrative inefficiency. It may reflect different interests among public actors. Thus, the national government may prioritize investment, exports and foreign exchange income. In contrast, regional or local governments usually face more directly the costs of mining activity: environmental impacts, water use, traffic, pressure on public services or conflicts with communities.

On the other hand, this institutional dimension acquires particular relevance when investment projects involve the expansion of both strategic infrastructure and trade corridors. Infrastructure and trade routes are not only economic elements, but can also acquire a strategic dimension by being linked to power relations, interests and diverse actors. In this sense, the expansion of new logistics corridors can generate economic opportunities. However, as a counterpart it can also increase exposure to activities linked to transnational organized crime, especially when there are weaknesses in control and supervision mechanisms. The literature on criminal governance has historically shown that criminal gangs can gradually permeate their influence into high-demand activities by exerting pressure on productive sectors, particularly in contexts where institutional weaknesses exist. Thus, in the mining projects analyzed above we consider it of vital importance to take into account political-institutional risk, which should not be limited to the possibility of social conflicts or regulatory delays, but should also incorporate the possibility that certain criminal actors attempt to take advantage of new flows of goods, infrastructure and hiring opportunities to insert themselves into segments of the international trade logistics chain.

In this sense, the Pacific corridor is substantial for understanding the risk of port investment projects, given that in 2025 cocaine seizures reported within its territory by Colombia (445.9 tonnes), Ecuador (75.9) and Chile (3.6) exceed 500 metric tonnes; Peru's figures are not comparable, as they measure destroyed rather than seized drugs. Pacific ports are exposed to illicit markets that increase violence rates within the cities that host them. This is evidenced, for example, in Callao, where 165 homicides were recorded through October 2025, a historic maximum compared to 146 for all of 2024, or in Valparaíso, the coastal region, where accumulated homicides and femicides increased 22% year-on-year (72 cases versus 59) at the end of September 2026.

Regarding drug trafficking, logistics and transport links are the most coveted elements by criminal organizations. This is due to the profitability they generate, and therefore, where organized crime competes most violently. Therefore, although the main threat regarding organized crime is the co-optation of strategic and logistics infrastructure, the sabotage capacity it has by increasing violence rates should not be overlooked, which may have implications in the future for the beneficiary's trust, and above all, for investment risk.

## 3.5. Asia-Pacific geopolitical dimension and strategic relevance of mining corridors

The data presented in the preceding tables, as well as what was previously stated about logistics and transport links, allow us to observe that the copper and lithium mining projects developed in Chile, Peru and Argentina are not only extractive initiatives oriented toward export. Rather, they are part of a logistics network integrated into the economic space of Asia-Pacific and at the same time coveted by criminal organizations. The configuration of the mine-to-port corridors shows that the ports of the South American Pacific coast acquire growing strategic importance due to their relative proximity to the main consumer markets for critical minerals, particularly China, South Korea, Japan and other industrial economies of East Asia.

From this perspective, the ports of Antofagasta, Mejillones, San Antonio, Callao and especially Chancay cease to be simple export departure points and become logistics nodes of strategic importance. The evidence collected shows that several Argentine mining projects use Chilean Pacific ports to access Asian markets, a situation that increases the relevance of Chilean logistics infrastructure within regional value chains. In turn, the development of the Chancay Megaport in Peru strengthens the capacity for direct connection with Asia, reducing logistics times and costs for South American exports.

In terms of strategic inference, the data suggest that the logistics bottlenecks identified in ports, roads and rail connections represent not only economic restrictions, but also limitations on the region's capacity to take advantage of the opportunities derived from growing Asian demand for critical minerals. Consequently, investments aimed at increasing port capacity, improving land connectivity and strengthening the security of logistics chains have an impact that transcends the commercial sphere, contributing to positioning South America as a relevant actor within the economic and geopolitical architecture of Asia-Pacific.

## 3.6. Opportunities for companies and investors

The economic impact of the Port of Chancay generates opportunities for complementary investments in storage, land transport, industrial parks, distribution centers, customs services and digital solutions for supply chain management. Likewise, it is observed that the coexistence of Chancay and Callao favors a redistribution of cargo traffic that can improve the efficiency of the national logistics system, expanding the investments with the greatest growth potential toward those aimed at strengthening land connectivity, specialized storage, multimodal logistics and value-added services linked to foreign trade.

For Chile, the opportunity lies in accelerating the agenda of operational efficiency and intermodal connectivity (rail and road) in San Antonio and Valparaíso to mitigate the difference in sailing times to the Asian market. Likewise, it is critical to advance the bill on the review of strategic Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) that is being processed in the Senate.

Despite the risks, the flow map reveals three clear opportunities for foreign capital:

**a) Immediate port CAPEX (2026–2030)**

The ports with the greatest potential return are:

- **Matarani:** structural saturation due to copper projects.
- **Callao – Concentrates Terminal:** critical gap for new projects.
- **Antofagasta / Mejillones:** pressure from Argentine projects dependent on Chilean ports.

**b) M&A in projects with secured off-takers**

The Rincón case (Rio Tinto + IFC) demonstrates that projects with secured contracts and institutional backing are the most attractive for mergers and acquisitions. According to the IFC, the project contemplates US$2,500 million in total investment, with a direct loan of US$400 million from the IFC, within a financing package of US$1,175 million. This type of financial structure reduces risk for investors, improves the project's bankability and positions assets with secured off-takers as priority candidates for M&A in the region.

**c) Vertical logistics integration**

Funds and companies can capture value through collection centers, dedicated corridors and participation in specialized terminals for lithium carbonate and hydroxide.

## 3.7. Conclusions and geostrategic perspective

The Andean corridor is going through a phase of accelerated expansion driven by lithium, copper and new energy flows, but the available logistics infrastructure is not growing at the pace of projected demand. Empirical data show that port bottlenecks, especially in Matarani, Callao, Antofagasta and Mejillones, will become the main limiting factor for regional competitiveness during the next decade. To this is added a fragmented and heterogeneous permitting system that increases execution times and raises regulatory risk in any Due Diligence process.

In this context, projects that integrate dedicated logistics, associated port CAPEX and secured off-taker contracts will be the ones that capture the most significant competitive advantages vis-à-vis Asian and North American markets. Finally, the evidence suggests that cross-border cooperation between Chile, Argentina and Peru will be indispensable to reduce dependence on single nodes, stabilize operational continuity and sustain the export expansion of the Andean corridor.
